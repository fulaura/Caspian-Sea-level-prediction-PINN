Abstract
The Caspian Sea is a closed inland basin whose water level depends on the balance
between atmospheric forcing, river inflow, evaporation, and basin storage, and reliable pre-
diction is complicated by data fragmentation across satellite, reanalysis, river-discharge,
and land-surface products. This study integrates ERA5, ERA5-Land, GLDAS, DAHITI,
and Volga discharge data into a unified hydroclimatic dataset covering 1993–2026 and
uses it to develop and compare three Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) formula-
tions: a water-balance PINN (PINN-1), a Budyko-constrained runoff-partitioning PINN
(PINN-2), and a Penman/Priestley–Taylor energy-balance PINN (PINN-3), all sharing a
CNN–LSTM backbone, against a no-physics CNN–LSTM baseline trained under identical
conditions. Daily, 10-day, and monthly resolutions were tested; the daily scale was unstable
and excluded, while monthly aggregation proved most reliable. The no-physics baseline
achieved the lowest monthly RMSE (0.0192 m), whereas PINN-3 achieved the highest
correlation (0.8279) and R² (0.6841), and a five-seed test confirmed PINN-3 stability
(RMSE = 0.0486 ± 0.0003). Because the 2018–2026 test period corresponds to a stronger
declining regime than most of the training period, absolute water-level scores partly
reflect trend-following ability, and the more conservative ∆H metrics provide a stricter
indication of predictive skill. The contribution is twofold: a reproducible multi-source
data-integration pipeline for closed-basin modelling, and an empirical demonstration that
physics-informed constraints improve pattern-level agreement but do not automatically
outperform a strong neural baseline on RMSE.
Keywords: Caspian Sea level, Physics-Informed Neural Networks, PINN, CNN–LSTM,
water balance, Budyko, Penman–Monteith, ERA5, GLDAS, DAHITI.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Background
Caspian Sea level changes are a major climate-related problem with serious impacts on
the environment, economy, and society. As the world’s largest inland body of water, the
Caspian Sea is not connected to the ocean; its water level depends on regional weather
patterns, river flows, and how water and energy move within the basin. Monthly changes
are mainly caused by rainfall on the lake surface, evaporation, river inflow, and seasonal
water storage in the surrounding land. Recent satellite measurements show a worrying
trend: the water level has already dropped by about 1.5 m between 1996 and 2021, mainly
because of less water coming from the Volga River and higher evaporation caused by
rising temperatures [8]. Climate projection studies based on CMIP6 simulations indicate
that the Caspian Sea level may continue to decline substantially under intermediate and
high-emission scenarios during the twenty-first century [7].
Predicting these changes is methodologically difficult. Standard machine learning methods
can be accurate in the short term, but they often ignore the laws of physics and may fail
as the climate continues to shift. Traditional physical models are more transparent, but
they can be unreliable when input data is uncertain or oversimplified. Existing research
also lacks a unified framework that can represent Caspian Sea dynamics at a monthly
scale while maintaining physically meaningful relationships between atmospheric forcing,
basin inflow, and evaporation.
A practical challenge is data fragmentation: relevant information is scattered across
different sources with inconsistent resolutions and formats. This creates a need for

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which combine the flexibility of AI with
explicit physical constraints. The object of this study is the monthly water-level dy-
namics of the Caspian Sea as represented through hydroclimatic, hydrological, and
satellite-altimetry observations. The subject is the development of physically constrained
neural-network models that preserve water-balance, runoff-partitioning, and evaporation
relationships. The current state of the problem shows that climate projections, reanalysis-
based hydrological methods, and satellite-based machine learning each provide useful
insight, but they are often applied separately.
1.2
Aim and Method
The aim of this research is to develop and apply Physics-Informed Neural Network
(PINN) models to predict variations and future fluctuations of the Caspian Sea level
by embedding physically meaningful hydrological and energy-based constraints into the
learning process. By incorporating water balance, basin-scale inflow partitioning, and
energy-constrained evaporation formulations, the study seeks to improve the physical
consistency, stability, and predictive reliability of closed sea level forecasts.
The objectives of the thesis are:
• to construct a consistent monthly master dataset by integrating ERA5, ERA5-Land,
GLDAS, and DAHITI sources over Caspian Sea coordinates;
• to derive and harmonize physically meaningful atmospheric, hydrological, and
lake-level variables from 1993 until 2026;
• to develop and train three PINN formulations with distinct physical constraints;
• to establish a comparative analytical framework for assessing how alternative
physical structures influence Caspian Sea level modeling.
The method of the study consists of constructing a unified monthly dataset, deriving
diagnostic variables, and comparing three PINN formulations. The first formulation
is based on lake water balance, the second introduces basin–lake coupling through
Budyko-constrained runoff partitioning, and the third constrains evaporation through an
energy-aware Penman–Monteith structure.
The main research questions:
• What data integration and harmonization strategies are required to transform

dataset for physics-informed learning?
• How does the systematic enforcement of different physical constraints — specifically

• To what extent does a multi-constrained PINN architecture provide additional
physical structure and interpretability compared with unconstrained machine

1.3
Basis and Scope
The theoretical basis of the research includes closed-basin hydrology, water-balance
modeling, Budyko-type runoff partitioning, Penman–Monteith evaporation theory, and
physics-informed machine learning. The methodological basis is formed by multi-source
data harmonization, monthly time-series modeling, feature derivation from reanalysis and
satellite products, and comparative evaluation of physically constrained neural-network
architectures.
This study provides methodological value by addressing both data integration and
structural modeling challenges. On the data side, it establishes a reproducible monthly
integration pipeline that synchronizes atmospheric reanalysis, land-surface hydrology,
and satellite altimetry products. From a modeling perspective, the research compares
three physically grounded PINN designs, each targeting a different component of the
same closed-basin system. This approach supports physical structure and interpretability
while retaining more flexibility than rigid deterministic formulations.
Several limitations are recognized. The study integrates sources with different temporal
resolutions, spatial grids, and variable conventions, so harmonization may introduce
minor uncertainty. Data acquisition was also limited by repository extraction speeds,
and defining the correct lake and basin coordinates required trial and error. Finally, the
monthly resolution supports long-term climate modeling but smooths short-term events
such as extreme weather surges or rapid discharge changes.

Chapter 2
Literature Review and Theoretical
Foundations
2.1
Conceptual Foundations
Research on Caspian Sea level prediction brings together several areas that are usually
studied separately: climate science, hydrology, remote sensing, and scientific machine
learning. For this thesis, the relevant literature is therefore not only the group of studies
written directly about the Caspian Sea. It also includes work on satellite and reanalysis
products, because these data sources are needed to describe water-level variability, and
work on physics-informed or physics-guided learning, because these methods explain how
physical knowledge can be added to data-driven models [7].
One important idea in this literature is that “physics-informed” is not one fixed method.
Raissi et al. present the classical PINN approach, where the residual of a governing
differential equation is added to the neural-network loss function [6]. Huang et al. use a
different type of physical guidance by adding geostrophic balance and spatial masking to
sea-surface-height prediction [1]. Bhasme et al. apply the same general idea in hydrology,
where consistency is based more on water-balance logic and process structure than on a
complete partial differential equation [9]. Although these studies use different technical
designs, they share the same basic principle: the model should not learn only from
statistical patterns, but should also be guided by what is already known about the
physical system [6].
This point matters for the Caspian Sea because its level is not controlled by one simple

variable. It is a closed basin, so changes in level depend on the combined effects of basin
runoff, precipitation over the sea, evaporation from the surface, terrestrial storage, and
long-term climate forcing [7]. For that reason, reviewing the literature only as a list of
prediction models would be incomplete. The more important question is which physical
assumptions are reliable, which datasets can represent the main processes, and which
modeling approaches can work with noisy or incomplete observations while still remaining
interpretable [8].
Overall, the reviewed studies suggest that a useful Caspian Sea forecasting framework
needs four main elements: basin-scale climate forcing, calibration against observations,
hydrologically meaningful constraints, and a learning model that is flexible enough to
handle nonlinearity and missing or imperfect data [7]. Since these elements are rarely
combined in one Caspian-specific model, the problem remains open and appropriate for
this thesis [9].
2.2
Hydroclimatic Context
The physical setting of the Caspian Sea itself explains much of the forecasting challenge.
Samant and Prange describe the Caspian drainage basin as roughly ten times larger than
the sea surface and fed by approximately 130 rivers, with the Volga River supplying
the dominant share of total inflow [7]. In such a system, sea-level change cannot be
interpreted as a purely local coastal process. It is the integrated outcome of atmospheric
forcing, land-surface hydrology, river inflow, and lake-surface evaporation across a vast
and climatically heterogeneous basin [7].
The climate projection literature further shows that the problem extends well beyond
short-term variability. According to Samant and Prange, projected increases in evaporation
are expected to exceed projected increases in precipitation across the basin during the
twenty-first century, resulting in a progressively more negative water balance [7]. Their
CMIP6-based analysis suggests substantial end-of-century water-level decline under
both intermediate and high-emission scenarios [7]. These projected changes imply major
ecological, economic, and infrastructural consequences, including shoreline transformation,
disruption of navigation, fishery impacts, and stress on coastal systems [7].
That same study highlights an additional modeling issue: the importance of physically
realistic lake representation. Several CMIP6 models had to be excluded because the

Caspian Sea surface was represented incorrectly, producing distorted surface area and
evaporation estimates [7]. For a closed basin, such geometric representation is not a minor
technical detail. Surface area directly affects the fluxes that determine the water balance,
implying that forecasting systems should not assume fixed lake area when substantial
water-level change is expected [7].
At shorter forecast horizons, Eftekhari et al. show that machine-learning methods applied
to GRACE and GRACE-FO observations can extract useful water-level signals for the
southern Caspian coast [3]. Their results demonstrate the predictive value of remote
sensing, but also reveal the limitations of unconstrained machine learning. A model
may reproduce past water-level patterns without clarifying the hydrological mechanisms
producing those patterns [3]. Under conditions of climatic nonstationarity, this limitation
becomes particularly important [3].
2.3
Caspian-Specific Studies Based on Climate and
Remote Sensing
Among Caspian-specific studies, Samant and Prange provide the clearest large-scale
assessment of the long-term decline problem [7]. Their contribution lies not only in
estimating future water-level reduction, but also in the selective treatment of the CMIP6
ensemble. Rather than averaging all models indiscriminately, they exclude models with
weak regional performance or unrealistic Caspian representation [7]. This improves the
credibility of their projections. However, the study remains a scenario-based climate
assessment rather than an operational forecasting system. It identifies the dominant
hydroclimatic pressures, but does not provide a framework capable of continuously
assimilating new observations, adapting to recent dynamics, or explicitly resolving short-
term fluctuations [7].
Eftekhari et al. occupy the opposite end of the methodological spectrum [3]. Their
study is more local, more operational, and more directly predictive. Using GRACE and
GRACE-FO observations for the period 2003-2023 and validating against the Anzali Port
tide-gauge record, they compare Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Multivariate Adaptive
Regression Splines [3]. The MARS model achieves the best predictive performance and is
then extended for forecasting to 2040 [3]. This study is important because it shows that
remote-sensing data alone can support meaningful regional prediction. At the same time,

structure, runoff-generation constraints, or evaporation limits, and its validation remains
geographically narrow relative to the full scale of the Caspian Sea [3].
Taken together, these two studies point toward the same broader conclusion. The climate-
projection literature shows that long-term decline is driven by worsening water balance,
especially increasing evaporation [7]. The satellite-based machine-learning literature
shows that observational data contain strong predictive signals [3]. What remains missing
is a modeling framework that combines these two insights by learning from data while
preserving the hydrological structure of a closed basin [7].
2.4
Closed-Basin Hydrology and Water-Balance
Modeling
The water-balance literature is especially relevant because the Caspian Sea is a closed
basin. In such systems, water-level variation is inseparable from storage accounting and
flux partitioning [8]. Saraceni et al. propose a reconstruction procedure combining ERA5-
Land variables with observed lake-level data to reproduce historical level changes in closed
lakes [8]. Their results show that relatively short observation records can still be useful
when paired with reanalysis-based estimates of precipitation, runoff, and evaporation [8].
This is both methodologically and practically important, as it shows that reanalysis can
provide continuous forcing while direct observations remain necessary for calibration and
drift correction [8].
Saraceni et al. are also explicit about the limits of their approach [8]. Occasional mis-
matches in reconstructed water balance are attributed mainly to precipitation biases in
ERA5-Land [8]. This is an important lesson for Caspian research: physically structured
forcing data are valuable, but should not be treated as ground truth. Instead, they are
best used as one component of a broader fused evidence framework that includes direct
observations [8].
Niedda et al. offer a longer-term physically based analysis through simulation of a closed
catchment-lake system in a semi-arid Mediterranean environment over 85 years [5]. Their
study combines rainfall-runoff modeling with energy-budget reasoning and shows that
precipitation is the dominant control on multi-year streamflow variability, while land-use
change affects hydrological behavior through changes in soil properties and catchment

2.5
Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Scientific
Machine Learning
Raissi et al. remain the foundational reference for strict physics-informed neural networks
[6]. In their framework, a neural network is trained not only to fit observed data, but
also to minimize the residual of the governing differential equation [6]. This formulation
introduced a new paradigm in which scientific computing becomes a joint optimization
problem balancing empirical fit and physical consistency [6]. Its relevance lies not only
in methodological novelty, but also in its suitability for systems where observations are
sparse while governing laws are partially known [6].
Cuomo et al. provide an important review of this literature while emphasizing that PINNs
are not a complete solution in themselves [11]. Their study documents the growth of PINN
variants and identifies several unresolved issues, including weak theoretical guarantees,
optimization instability, sensitivity to activation functions, and the difficulty of balancing
multiple loss terms [11]. This is especially relevant for environmental applications, where
model training may involve simultaneous data-fit, equation, boundary-condition, and
regularization losses [11]. The inclusion of physics is therefore not automatically beneficial;
its effectiveness depends strongly on how constraints are specified and weighted [11].
Podina et al. extend the field through Universal PINNs, which combine known operator
structure with a neural component representing unknown terms and then use symbolic
regression to recover hidden dynamics [2]. This approach is particularly relevant for
hydrology and climate applications, where the governing physical structure is often
incomplete rather than fully absent [2]. Their work shows that physics-informed learning
can serve not only as a predictive framework, but also as a tool for discovering missing
structure in sparse and noisy systems [2].

Huang et al. provide one of the most useful transfer cases for future Caspian appli-

2.6
Physics-Guided Hydrological Learning and
Budyko-Based Constraints
Bhasme et al. move away from strict PDE-residual PINNs and instead develop a physics-
informed machine-learning framework grounded in conceptual hydrological structure [9].
Their model is designed to predict streamflow and actual evapotranspiration while preserv-
ing physical consistency in the water balance [9]. This is highly relevant to the Caspian
problem because it illustrates a practical middle path between rigid mechanistic modeling
and unconstrained statistical learning [9]. Rather than requiring a complete differential
specification of the full system, the model constrains learning through hydrological logic
and physically plausible intermediate states [9].
Esmaeilzadeh and Amirzadeh reinforce the relevance of this framework through replication
[4]. Using the original data structure and code logic, they show that the physics-guided
approach can provide additional physical structure and interpretability relative to stan-
dalone conceptual and standalone machine-learning models in monthly hydrological
prediction [4]. Although replication is not equivalent to a new theoretical contribution, it
provides evidence that the hybrid framework is reproducible rather than accidental [4].
Cheng et al. bring the Budyko framework directly into machine learning by using it as
a runoff-partitioning constraint at global scale [10]. Their results show that runoff and
baseflow estimation improve when learning is restricted to a physically plausible parti-
tioning regime [10]. This is important because Budyko-based theory provides hydrological
meaning without requiring a full mechanistic simulation of every process pathway [10].
Liu et al. advance this argument further by estimating time-varying parameters in a

2.7
Methodological Comparison
Across the source set, four broad methodological families can be identified. The first
is climate-system modeling, represented by Samant and Prange [7]. This approach is
strongest for long-horizon scenario analysis, regional forcing assessment, and understand-
ing hydroclimatic tendencies under emission pathways [7]. Its main limitation is structural
error: if lake geometry, surface area, or evaporation processes are poorly represented,
physically misleading projections may result [7].
The second family is observational machine learning based on satellite and gauge records,
represented by Eftekhari et al. [3]. These models are often easier to implement, can
perform well on limited datasets, and provide direct predictive value [3]. Their main
limitations are weaker interpretability and possible fragility under nonstationarity, since
they learn associations in the historical record without necessarily preserving hydrological
logic [3].
The third family is water-balance reconstruction and physically based hydrological
simulation, represented by Saraceni et al. and Niedda et al. [8]. These methods are
strongest when explanation matters, because precipitation, evaporation, runoff, and
storage are kept explicitly linked [8]. Their main weakness lies in dependence on forcing-
data quality and on the availability of observed level or discharge records for calibration
[8].
The fourth family is physics-informed or physics-guided machine learning, which is
the most heterogeneous group [11]. Raissi et al. represent strict differential-equation
residual PINNs [6]. Huang et al. represent domain-specific physical regularization inside
a neural forecasting model [1]. Bhasme et al. represent conceptual hydrological guidance
[9]. Cheng et al. represent theory-constrained learning through Budyko partitioning [10].
This diversity should not be interpreted as inconsistency. Rather, it shows that physical
knowledge can be embedded into learning at different depths depending on the reliability

2.8
Main Findings
Several conclusions appear consistently across the reviewed studies. The first is that
water-level change in a closed basin is mainly a problem of flux partitioning. Samant and
Prange connect the long-term decline of the Caspian Sea to evaporation rising faster than
precipitation [7]. Closed-lake and hydrological studies make a related point: over several
years, variability depends on how precipitation is divided among evapotranspiration,
runoff, and storage [5]. Budyko-based research gives this idea a more formal theoretical
basis by showing that the central question is not only how much water enters a basin,
but what happens to that water after it arrives [10].
A second finding is that physical constraints are useful only when they match the
dominant processes and are not imposed too strongly. Huang et al. demonstrate that
geostrophic regularization can improve sea-surface-height forecasting, but they also show
that overly strong constraints can reduce predictive performance [1]. Cuomo et al. discuss
the same issue more generally, identifying loss balancing as a continuing challenge in
PINN research [11]. This means that adding physics to a model is not automatically
beneficial. The constraint must be relevant to the system, and its influence during training
must be calibrated carefully [11].
A third pattern is that sparse or imperfect data do not rule out scientific machine
learning, although they do affect what kind of model can be trusted. Podina et al. show
that operator discovery can still work with limited data when the model has some
prior structure [2]. Saraceni et al. similarly show that short observational records can
support hydrological reconstruction when they are combined with reanalysis forcing and
a mass-balance framework [8]. For this thesis, the implication is that data scarcity should
be treated as a design constraint, not as a reason to abandon physically guided modeling
altogether [2].
Finally, the literature suggests that hydrological parameters should not always be assumed
constant. Liu et al. show that monthly Budyko partitioning parameters change over time
as climatic and basin conditions shift [12]. For a rapidly changing system such as the
Caspian Sea, this finding is especially important because it challenges the use of fixed
rainfall–runoff–evapotranspiration relationships in long-term prediction [12].

2.9
Research Gaps
The most direct gap is the lack of a well-established physics-informed neural network
designed specifically for the Caspian Sea. The reviewed literature includes climate
projections, satellite-based prediction studies, water-balance reconstruction methods, and
broader PINN or hydrological hybrid frameworks. However, these strands are usually
treated separately, and no single model clearly brings them together for Caspian Sea
level prediction [7].
Another gap concerns the connection between scales. Existing studies range from global
climate projections to local coastal prediction, basin hydrology, and general machine-
learning theory [3]. What is still missing is a framework that links catchment runoff
generation, basin water balance, and whole-sea level response while still remaining
interpretable [9].
Dynamic geometry is also underrepresented. Samant and Prange show that changes in lake
surface area and bathymetry influence evaporation and therefore feed back into the water
balance itself [7]. Many predictive approaches still rely on fixed-geometry assumptions or
simplified storage-to-level conversion. These simplifications may be acceptable for small
fluctuations, but they become more problematic when water-level change is large [7].
There is also an unresolved problem of combining multiple data sources while keeping
uncertainty explicit. Reanalysis products, satellite altimetry, gravimetry, discharge ob-
servations, and gauge records each describe different parts of the Caspian system and
each carries its own errors [8]. The literature provides relatively few examples of one
framework that can integrate these sources while making uncertainty visible rather than
hiding it inside the model [8].
Constraint selection and weighting remain another important gap. The PINN review
literature and the South China Sea forecasting study both show that physics-informed
models depend strongly on how their losses are defined and balanced [11]. This difficulty
is likely to be even greater in hydroclimatic systems, where physical relationships can
vary by season, region, and hydrological state [1].
Finally, many forecasting systems still assume hydrological relationships that are fixed
or only weakly variable. The monthly Budyko study by Liu et al. suggests that runoff
partitioning parameters can evolve over time [12]. In a drying closed basin exposed to

long-term forecasting [12].
2.10
Synthesis
Taken as a whole, the literature points toward a clear research direction. Caspian-specific
studies show that the basin is already under strong hydroclimatic stress, so models need
to address both long-term decline and shorter-term fluctuations [7]. Closed-lake and
water-balance studies show that an interpretable model must keep precipitation, inflow,
evaporation, and storage connected rather than treating them as unrelated predictors
[8]. The core PINN literature explains how physical laws can be embedded directly
into learning [6], while hydrological hybrid and Budyko-based studies show that useful
physical guidance does not always require a fully specified differential-equation system
[9].
The strongest conclusion from the reviewed work is that a future Caspian forecasting
framework should avoid both extremes: it should not be a purely black-box statistical
model, but it also should not be an overly rigid process model. A more suitable approach
would combine multi-source observations with constraints from closed-basin water balance,
basin inflow partitioning, and evaporation dynamics. At the same time, the model should
allow these relationships to change as climate and basin conditions change [12]. This
unresolved space provides the main justification for the modeling approach developed in
this thesis [7].

3.1
Research Design Overview
This study follows a quantitative model-development and comparative evaluation design
structured around two coupled tasks: (i) construction of a unified monthly hydroclimatic
dataset for the Caspian Sea system and (ii) formulation and comparison of three physics-
informed neural network (PINN) configurations for modeling monthly Caspian Sea level
dynamics. The workflow is designed to preserve physical meaning during spatial and
temporal aggregation, maintain consistency across heterogeneous data sources, and embed
process-based constraints into model training.
The methodological logic of the study is based on the view that reliable prediction of
closed-basin water-level dynamics cannot rely only on unconstrained statistical fitting.
Instead, model design should combine observation-based learning with hydrologically
meaningful representations of precipitation, evaporation, inflow generation, energy bal-
ance, and seasonal variability [6]. Accordingly, the dataset was assembled to support three
complementary formulations: a lake water-balance PINN, a basin–lake coupled PINN
constrained by Budyko partitioning, and an energy-aware evaporation PINN constrained
through Penman–Monteith structure [9].

3.2.1
Source Structure
The master dataset integrates variables from atmospheric reanalysis, land-surface reanal-
ysis, hydrological products, satellite altimetry, and derived diagnostic terms. ERA5 and
ERA5-Land provide the main atmospheric and energy-balance forcing variables [13] [14].
GLDAS products provide basin-scale terrestrial hydrological variables, including snow-

accuracy of PINN2 model, which includes Budyko formula, Volga river discharge data
was added [18]. DAHITI provides the primary supervisory target for monthly lake-level
modeling [17]. In addition, several variables were derived analytically from the raw fields
in order to support physically constrained model design.
3.2.2
ERA5 Lake-Scale Atmospheric Variables
ERA5 daily statistics on single levels were collected for the Caspian Sea spatial domain
and processed into monthly lake-scale predictors [13]. The selected lake variables include
precipitation over the lake surface (Plake), lake evaporation (Elake), 2 m air temperature
(T2mlake), 2 m dew point temperature (Td2mlake), 10 m zonal wind (U10lake), 10 m
meridional wind (V 10lake), surface pressure (SPlake), and net radiation over the lake
(Rnlake).
The raw ERA5 lake-scale inputs were extracted from the Copernicus Climate Data Store
using the cdsapi Python interface. The request domain was defined by the Caspian
Sea lake bounding box, with northern, western, southern, and eastern limits of 47.5,
45.5, 36.5, and 54.5 degrees, respectively. Data were requested for the 1993–2026 period
on a month-by-month basis. To preserve the distinction between state variables and
accumulated fluxes, two separate requests were made: one for daily mean fields, including 2
m temperature, 2 m dew point temperature, 10 m zonal and meridional wind components,
and surface pressure; and another for daily sum fields, including total precipitation,
evaporation, surface net solar radiation, and surface net thermal radiation. All files
were requested in NetCDF format and saved as monthly zipped NetCDF archives, with
separate outputs for daily mean and daily sum variables. Existing monthly files were
skipped during extraction to avoid duplicate downloads. This step produced the raw
lake-scale ERA5 atmospheric inputs used later for preprocessing, monthly aggregation,
variable derivation, and model training.

enough to retain regional differences across the northern, central, and southern Caspian
Sea, but not so fine that preprocessing becomes unnecessarily heavy for repeated model
preparation. This step was important because hydroclimatic conditions vary substantially
across the Caspian region and should not be collapsed too early into a single spatial
average.
After metric conversion, masking, and gridding, the ERA5, ERA5-Land, and GLDAS
gridded data streams were aligned along common time coordinates and stored in unified
NetCDF (.nc) structures. NetCDF was used because it is well suited for multidimensional
geospatial data: it preserves time, latitude, longitude, variable names, units, and metadata
in one structured file, making masking, gridding, and later feature extraction more
convenient and reproducible than storing these fields as flat tables. DAHITI lake-level
observations were kept in a separate dedicated CSV file, and Volga river-discharge data
were also stored in a separate CSV file. These CSV-based observational streams were then
time-aligned with the NetCDF predictors during feature construction and training-dataset
assembly. Where incomplete or inconsistent values were identified in one source stream,
only the affected stream was reprocessed and reintegrated, preserving reproducibility
while avoiding unnecessary changes to already validated components.
The final preprocessing output consisted of three similar but temporally different training-
ready datasets. The first version was daily and preserved records for each available day.
The second version used 10-day aggregation, where state variables were averaged and
flux variables were summed over each 10-day period; this structure was created to align
more closely with DAHITI observations, which provide lake-level values at approximately
10-day intervals presented in the Figure 3.4. The third version was monthly, with state
variables represented by monthly averages and flux variables by monthly sums. These
parallel datasets were prepared so that model performance could be compared across daily,
10-day, and monthly temporal resolutions, after which the best-performing resolution
could be selected for the final PINN training experiments.

3.6
Training and Comparative Evaluation Design
All three PINNs share the same monthly target variable and common data foundation.
The dataset is divided chronologically into training, validation, and testing subsets in
order to preserve temporal causality and avoid information leakage across periods. This
chronological design is especially important for hydroclimatic forecasting because future
states should not influence parameter learning from earlier periods.
Comparative evaluation is designed to examine three dimensions of model behavior:
predictive accuracy, physical consistency, and interpretability. Predictive performance
will be assessed using standard regression metrics such as root mean squared error
(RMSE), correlation, and coefficient of determination (R2). Temporal plots of observed
and predicted lake-level trajectories will also be used to assess the ability of each
formulation to reconstruct seasonal and multi-year variability.
Physical consistency will be evaluated by examining the extent to which each model
respects its imposed hydrological or energy-based constraint during prediction. For
PINN-1, the primary issue is balance consistency between storage change and the flux
terms. For PINN-2, the focus is whether runoff generation remains compatible with
Budyko partitioning under changing climatic conditions. For PINN-3, the key issue is
whether evaporation behavior remains physically plausible when constrained through
energy-balance logic. This comparative design is intended to determine not only which
model predicts best numerically, but also which formulation remains most physically
credible under incomplete and heterogeneous observations [11].
The evaluation design followed a two-stage structure. First, PINN-2 was used as a
reference screening model to compare daily, 10-day, and monthly temporal resolutions
because its Budyko-based formulation directly represents basin-scale runoff partitioning
and was therefore suitable for testing temporal aggregation effects. After the unsuitable
daily resolution was excluded, the 10-day and monthly resolutions were retained for
cross-model comparison. The PINN-2 results reported in the final comparison are the
same reference-formulation results used during this screening stage. Therefore, PINN-2

To isolate the contribution of the physics-informed loss term, an additional ablation

initialization, data-loader order, and dropout masks, varied between runs. This experiment
was used to evaluate whether the reported PINN-3 metrics were representative of the
formulation or strongly dependent on a single random initialization.
3.7
Methodological Limitations
Several limitations are recognized in advance. First, basin inflow representation relies
partly on proxy hydrological terms where continuous direct observations are incomplete.
Second, physically constrained loss design requires careful weighting in order to balance
data fit and process consistency, and poor weighting may reduce rather than improve
predictive performance [1]. Third, uncertainties in reanalysis-derived and model-derived
forcing variables may propagate into both the learned relationships and the interpretation
of physical consistency.
A further methodological limitation concerns the regime asymmetry of the chronological
train/validation/test split. Since the test period contains a pronounced declining trend in
Caspian Sea level, the target distribution differs from much of the training period. This
distribution shift may affect the interpretation of model performance: high agreement
in reconstructed absolute water level can partly reflect the model’s ability to follow the
35


================================================================================
PAGE 47 of 89
================================================================================

CHAPTER 3. METHODS
dominant downward trend, rather than fully demonstrate robust process-level forecasting
skill across all hydroclimatic regimes. For this reason, the results should be interpreted
together with the monthly ∆H metrics, the no-physics CNN–LSTM ablation baseline,
and the physical consistency discussion.
Another limitation is the use of a constant reference lake surface area Alake in the
storage-to-level conversion. This simplification is common for first-order closed-basin
water-balance modelling, but it is not fully realistic for the Caspian Sea because a
declining water level changes the exposed shoreline area and modifies the relationship
between storage change and level change. The effect is expected to be especially important
in the shallow northern Caspian, where small vertical changes can produce relatively
large horizontal shoreline shifts. As a result, the converted inflow contribution and
evaporation-related area effects may contain scaling uncertainty.

Therefore, even a surface-area change of only a few percent would produce a similar
relative scaling error in the converted inflow contribution Qvolga∆t/Alake. For example,

makes the same Volga discharge appear as a smaller water-level contribution than it
would under a dynamic area formulation. Since this scaling enters the physical residual
rather than the supervised data loss, it mainly affects the magnitude and calibration of
the physics-informed loss. It can therefore make exact agreement between the predicted
∆H and the physically estimated ∆H more difficult, without directly biasing the data-
fitting component of the neural network. A full dynamic A(H) or bathymetry-based
storage–elevation formulation is left for future work.

4.1
Experimental Setup
To evaluate the proposed physics-informed models, three temporal versions of the final
dataset were prepared: monthly, decadal (10-day), and daily. Before training all three
PINN variants, temporal resolution was first tested using PINN-2 as the reference model,
because the Budyko-based constraint is more sensitive to temporal aggregation and
can indicate whether the dataset preserves meaningful hydrological structure. This step
was used to determine which temporal resolutions were suitable for the final model
comparison.
After the temporal resolution selection, PINN-1, PINN-2, and PINN-3 were trained
separately on the selected datasets. The models were compared using the same evaluation
procedure, including prediction errors for water-level change and reconstruction accuracy
of the water-level trajectory. This setup allowed the comparison to reflect differences
in physical constraints and temporal resolution rather than changes in the evaluation
procedure.
4.2
Overview of PINN Models
All PINN configurations use the same spatio-temporal CNN–LSTM backbone. The
input is represented as a sequence of gridded hydrometeorological variables, where
each time step contains spatial fields describing lake and basin conditions. The CNN
block processes every time step separately and extracts compact spatial features from

variables such as precipitation, evaporation, runoff, snow-related indicators, atmospheric
variables, and river discharge. These CNN-derived feature vectors are then passed to the
LSTM, which captures temporal dependence across the selected look-back window and

the PINN models combines spatial feature extraction, temporal sequence modelling, and
physical regularization within one training framework.
4.2.1
PINN-1 Architecture and Experimental Setup
In this setup, the model does not only learn the statistical relationship between input
variables and observed water-level change. It also compares its predicted ∆H with a
physically estimated water-level change derived from lake precipitation, lake evaporation,
Volga discharge, and residual basin runoff.
The implemented PINN-1 architecture is shown in Figure 4.1. The model receives a
sequence of gridded input tensors with 13 atmospheric and hydrological channels over a
110 × 90 spatial grid. Before temporal modelling, the input sequence is reshaped so that
each time step can be passed through the convolutional feature extractor. The spatial
encoder consists of two convolutional blocks. The first convolutional layer maps the 13
input channels into 32 feature maps, followed by a ReLU activation and max-pooling
operation. The second convolutional layer increases the feature depth from 32 to 64
channels. After this, adaptive average pooling reduces the spatial representation to a
fixed 4 × 4 feature map, which produces 1024 spatial features for each time step.

The extracted spatial features are then passed into an LSTM layer, which processes
the temporal sequence and represents how the hydrometeorological conditions evolve
over time. This is necessary because Caspian Sea level change is not controlled only
by the current state of the atmosphere or basin, but also by previous precipitation,
evaporation, runoff, and discharge conditions. The final hidden representation from the
LSTM is passed through a fully connected regression block with dropout regularization.
The output layer produces six values, including the predicted water-level change and the
physics-related parameters used in the water-balance constraint.
During training, the model minimizes both the data loss between predicted and observed
∆H and the physics loss between ∆Hpred and ∆Hphys. Adaptive loss weighting is used
so that the model can balance observational fitting and physical consistency during
optimization. In addition, the predicted absolute water-level trajectory is reconstructed
by cumulatively summing the predicted ∆H values from the initial observed level. This

4.2.2
PINN-2 (Budyko-Constrained Model) Setup
For PINN-2, the shared CNN–LSTM structure is implemented with an input tensor
of shape (1, 6, 13, 110, 90), where the six time steps form the look-back window, the
13 channels represent gridded meteorological and hydrological variables, and 110 × 90
is the spatial resolution. The channels include precipitation, temperature, radiation,
pressure, evaporation, dewpoint temperature, wind components, runoff variables, snow
water equivalent, and Volga discharge. Before training, all variables are temporally aligned
to the selected resolution and normalized using statistics from the training split.
The six temporal slices are first reshaped so that each time step is passed through the
same CNN encoder. The encoder maps the 13 input channels to 32 feature maps using
a two-dimensional convolution, applies SiLU activation, and reduces the spatial size
from 110 × 90 to 55 × 45 through max pooling. A second convolution increases the
feature depth from 32 to 64 channels, followed by another SiLU activation and adaptive
average pooling to a fixed 4 × 4 representation. Thus, each time step is compressed into
64 × 4 × 4 = 1024 spatial features. These feature vectors are then restored into a six-step
sequence and passed to a two-layer LSTM with a hidden size of 64, allowing the model
to learn delayed hydrological responses from recent basin and lake conditions rather than
treating each observation independently.

4.2.3
PINN-3 (Penman-Based Evaporation Constraint) Setup
PINN-3 uses the same CNN–LSTM backbone as PINN-2: six time steps, 13 input
channels, and the identical convolution–pooling–LSTM block. This keeps the comparison
methodologically fair, while the regression head is modified to output four time-step
scalars instead of three: the normalized water-level change c
∆ht and three Priestley–Taylor
residual parameters.
The three physics outputs are αPT,t, constrained to αPT,t ≥1.0 with a softplus trans-
formation and unit offset; Cwind,t, constrained positive for aerodynamic transfer; and
γrunoff,t, constrained positive for residual basin runoff. The 13 channels include precipita-
tion, temperature, shortwave and longwave radiation, pressure, evaporation, dew-point
temperature, wind components, runoff, snow water equivalent, and Volga discharge; all
gridded variables are normalized per channel using training-split statistics.
Training uses AdamW (weight decay 0.05) with a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule
from 3 × 10−4, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and physics weight λphys = 1.0 — all
identical to PINN-2. The training horizon was set at 300 epochs with early stopping on
validation MSE (patience 50). Dropout of 0.5 is applied between the two LSTM layers
and inside the regressor head, matching PINN-2.
Figure 4.3 shows the schematic structure of the PINN-3 regression head. The shared
CNN–LSTM backbone (identical to Figure 4.2) terminates in a 64-dimensional vector
that feeds a two-layer fully-connected block. The block produces four outputs: a direct
linear output for the predicted water-level change, and three softplus-activated outputs
for the physics coefficients. The αPT,t output additionally receives a constant offset of
+1.0 to enforce the Priestley–Taylor lower bound. This four-head structure means PINN-3
does not only predict the target variable directly, but also estimates the internal physical
parameters used in the energy-balance residual.

4.3
Temporal Resolution Selection Using PINN-2
PINN-2 was selected as the temporal-resolution screening model because the Budyko
constraint links basin precipitation, potential evapotranspiration, runoff partitioning, and
lake-level response. This made it a suitable reference formulation for testing whether daily,
10-day, or monthly aggregation produced the most stable physically constrained learning
behaviour. The purpose of this stage was not to establish PINN-2 as an independent
baseline, but to identify which temporal resolutions were appropriate for the final cross-
model comparison.

Based on the temporal-resolution tests, the daily dataset was not selected for the final

the performance of every PINN formulation on both monthly and 10-day datasets.

Regime-Shift Interpretation of the Test Period
An important caveat in the interpretation of the final results is the hydroclimatic regime
shift between the training and testing periods. The chronological split was necessary to
avoid information leakage, but it also means that the model was trained mostly on earlier
dynamics and tested on a period characterized by a more persistent decline in Caspian
Sea level. Therefore, the test results represent performance under a non-stationary target
distribution rather than performance under a fully symmetric train–test regime.

This point is especially important for the reconstructed absolute water-level curves.
Since the 2018–2026 test period contains a strong downward trend, high absolute-level
reconstruction agreement can be partly supported by capturing the dominant trend itself.
Such metrics should therefore not be interpreted as complete proof that the model has
learned all hydrological mechanisms of regime change. Instead, the results are better
understood as evidence that the monthly models can reproduce the main declining
trajectory and part of the monthly variability under a shifted test regime.
For this reason, the comparative conclusions in this chapter emphasize relative behaviour
across formulations, monthly ∆H metrics, the no-physics ablation baseline, and physical
interpretability rather than claiming definitive forecasting robustness across all possi-
ble Caspian Sea regimes. A stronger validation design would require walk-forward or
expanding-window evaluation, where the training window is progressively extended and
models are repeatedly tested on later periods. This is left as a priority for future work.
A related issue is the difference between evaluating reconstructed absolute water level
H and evaluating monthly water-level change ∆H. Absolute-level reconstruction can
produce high R2 values when the model follows the dominant long-term trajectory of
the test period. In the 2018–2026 test interval, this trajectory is strongly downward, so
a high absolute-level R2 partly reflects trend-following ability. For this reason, the ∆H
metrics are more conservative indicators of short-term predictive skill. The monthly ∆H
results, where R2 values are approximately in the 0.6100–0.6800 range for the strongest
formulations, provide a stricter assessment of whether the models capture month-to-month
water-level variability rather than only reconstructing the overall declining path.
Constant-Area Assumption
The interpretation of the water-balance and evaporation-related results is also affected
by the constant-area assumption. In the implemented PINN formulations, Alake is used
as a fixed reference area for converting Volga discharge into an equivalent water-level
contribution. This allows all monthly physical terms to be expressed in metres and
compared with the predicted ∆H. However, the Caspian Sea is not geometrically static:
as the water level declines, surface area and shallow-water extent change, especially in
the northern basin. Therefore, the constant-area assumption may introduce a scaling
bias into the physical residual, particularly during periods of stronger level decline.
This limitation does not invalidate the comparison between the tested formulations,

Conceptual Overlap Between Runoff Terms
The interpretation of the model comparison also requires caution because the physical
formulations are not fully orthogonal. PINN-1 is primarily a lake water-balance model,
but its residual basin term γother max(0, Pbasin −PETbasin) introduces a simplified basin
water-surplus correction. This correction is conceptually related to the runoff-partitioning
problem addressed more explicitly by PINN-2. Therefore, the comparison between PINN-1
and PINN-2 should not be read as a completely clean separation between water-balance
physics and runoff-partitioning physics.
The more appropriate interpretation is that PINN-1 represents a direct water-balance
formulation with a coarse residual correction for unresolved basin inflow, whereas PINN-
2 makes basin runoff partitioning the central physical constraint through the Budyko
framework. This distinction explains why the models can share some hydrological intuition
while still testing different levels of physical structure. It also supports a cautious
interpretation of the comparative results: differences in performance reflect not only
different physical laws, but also different ways of representing basin contribution to
lake-level change.
Use of PINN-2 as a Reference Formulation
A methodological limitation concerns the dual role of PINN-2 in the experimental
workflow. PINN-2 was first used as the reference model for temporal-resolution screening
in Section 4.3, where daily, 10-day, and monthly resolutions were compared. The same
trained PINN-2 results at the selected 10-day and monthly resolutions were then retained
as the Budyko-constrained reference formulation in the final model comparison.
This design choice was made for continuity: PINN-2 provides the clearest basin-scale
runoff-partitioning constraint, making it suitable for testing the effect of temporal
aggregation. However, it also means that the PINN-2 metrics should not be interpreted as
independent post-selection validation evidence. The comparison among PINN-1, PINN-

4.8
Threats to Validity
This section consolidates the methodological limitations of the study that may affect
the interpretation of the reported metrics. The purpose is to make these threats explicit
in one place, rather than leave them scattered across the methodology and discussion
sections.
Target distribution shift in the test period. The chronological split preserves tempo-
ral causality and avoids information leakage, but the 2018–2026 test period corresponds
to a stronger declining regime than most of the 1993–2014 training period. As a result,
the test set is not a randomly sampled hold-out subset but an out-of-regime evaluation
window. High agreement on reconstructed absolute water level partly reflects the ability
to follow the dominant downward trajectory. The ∆H metrics are therefore the more
conservative indicators of month-to-month predictive skill.
Constant lake-area assumption. The storage-to-level conversion uses a fixed reference
Alake. As discussed in Section 3.7, even a surface-area change of only a few percent would
produce a similar relative scaling error in the converted inflow contribution Qvolga∆t/Alake.
This affects the magnitude and calibration of the physics-informed residual, but not the
supervised data loss.
Dual role of PINN-2. PINN-2 was used both for temporal-resolution screening and
as the Budyko-constrained reference formulation in the final comparison. The reported
metrics are valid within the chosen experimental design, but they are not equivalent to
independent post-selection validation evidence.
Conceptual overlap between PINN-1 and PINN-2. The residual basin-surplus
term in PINN-1 implements a simplified version of the runoff-partitioning logic made
explicit in PINN-2. The comparison should therefore be read as a comparison of different
levels of physical explicitness rather than of fully orthogonal physical mechanisms.
Fixed physics-loss weight. All physics-informed models were trained with λphys = 1.0.
A sensitivity analysis on this hyperparameter was not performed. As discussed in the
physics-informed forecasting literature, the relative weighting of data and physics losses
can substantially affect predictive behaviour. The present results should therefore be
interpreted under this fixed-weight assumption.
Multi-seed evaluation only for PINN-3. The stability of PINN-3 was verified across

five random seeds, but PINN-1, PINN-2, and the no-physics CNN–LSTM baseline were
each trained from a single random initialization. The numerical ranking of the four
models could therefore be partly influenced by initialization effects for the non-PINN-3
entries. Repeating the multi-seed analysis for all four models is identified as a priority
for future work.
Volga discharge as input feature versus explicit physical term. In PINN-2
and PINN-3, Volga discharge is provided as an input feature but not added explicitly
inside the physical residual to avoid double counting. This means the Volga signal enters
training mainly through the data-loss pathway, which leads to asymmetric regularization
compared with PINN-1, where Volga discharge appears explicitly in the water-balance
residual.
Source heterogeneity. The integrated dataset combines ERA5, ERA5-Land, GLDAS,
DAHITI, and GloFAS products, each with its own spatial grid, temporal resolution, and
uncertainty profile. The harmonization steps described in Section 3.3 reduce, but do not
eliminate, the impact of these inconsistencies on the physical residual.
Taken together, these threats do not invalidate the reported metrics, but they constrain
the strength of comparative claims that can be drawn from them. In particular, conclusions
about whether physics-informed learning is uniformly preferable to unconstrained neural
learning in closed-basin hydrology would require the validation design outlined in the
Conclusion as future work.

Chapter 5
Conclusion
This thesis makes two main contributions. First, it presents a reproducible multi-source
data integration pipeline that combines ERA5, ERA5-Land, GLDAS, DAHITI, and
Volga discharge data into unified monthly, 10-day, and daily datasets for closed-basin
hydroclimatic modelling of the Caspian Sea over 1993–2026. Second, it provides an em-
pirical comparison of three Physics-Informed Neural Network formulations, namely water
balance, Budyko-constrained runoff partitioning, and Penman/Priestley–Taylor energy
balance, against a no-physics CNN–LSTM baseline trained under identical conditions.
Together, these contributions provide a process-aware experimental framework for testing
when physics-informed learning is numerically beneficial in closed-basin level prediction
and when a strong unconstrained neural baseline remains competitive.
The main quantitative outcome of the work is that the no-physics CNN–LSTM baseline
achieved the lowest monthly RMSE (0.0192 m), while PINN-3 achieved the highest
correlation (0.8279) and R2 (0.6841) among all four monthly models. This result clarifies
an important methodological point for the broader physics-informed hydrology literature:
when the data-driven backbone already receives a rich set of atmospheric, hydrological,
and discharge inputs, the unconstrained network can capture a substantial part of the
statistical signal directly from data. Under these conditions, the numerical advantage
of adding a physics-informed loss term depends on careful calibration of loss weight-
ing, residual scaling, and the match between the imposed constraint and the observed
hydroclimatic regime.
The five-seed stability test of PINN-3 confirms that the reported ∆H performance is
not strongly dependent on a single random initialization (RMSE = 0.0486 ± 0.0003).

CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSION
The wider variability observed in absolute-level R2 across seeds further supports the
interpretation that ∆H metrics provide a more conservative measure of predictive skill
than reconstructed-level metrics, particularly under the declining test regime of 2018–
2026.
The temporal-resolution screening identified monthly aggregation as the most reliable
scale for physically constrained learning on this dataset. Daily resolution produced
unstable predictions and was excluded; 10-day resolution preserved some intermediate
variability but had limited explanatory power. This finding is itself a methodological
contribution: for closed-basin level modelling with the available reanalysis and altimetry
data, sub-monthly temporal resolutions amplify noise faster than they add usable physical
signal.
Several limitations remain. The constant reference lake-area assumption simplifies the
storage-to-level conversion and should be replaced in future work by a dynamic hypso-
metric relation or bathymetry-based storage–elevation curve. The comparison between
PINN-1 and PINN-2 should also be interpreted with caution because the residual basin-
surplus term in PINN-1 partially overlaps with the runoff-partitioning logic made explicit
in PINN-2. In addition, PINN-2 was used both for temporal-resolution screening and as
the Budyko-constrained reference formulation in the final comparison, so its reported
metrics should not be treated as independent post-selection validation evidence.
Overall, the thesis shows that physics-informed learning provides an interpretable struc-
tural framework for Caspian Sea level modelling, but it does not automatically guarantee
lower RMSE than a strong neural baseline, and a direct quantitative validation of the
learned physical coefficients against independent hydrological references is left as future
work. The most defensible conclusion is that the tested PINN formulations provide a
useful process-aware modelling framework, while their predictive advantage depends on
temporal resolution, loss calibration, residual scaling, and validation under changing
hydroclimatic regimes.
A broader extension of this work would involve a full experimental validation framework
beyond the scope of the present thesis. Such a framework should include repeated-
seed training for PINN-1, PINN-2, PINN-3, and the no-physics CNN–LSTM baseline;
retraining all formulations after fixing the temporal resolution; walk-forward or expanding-
window validation on non-stationary climate windows; sensitivity analysis of the physics-
loss weight λphys; dynamic lake geometry through an A(H) or bathymetry-based storage–
